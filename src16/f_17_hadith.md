## Nineteen

In 1974, a biochemist named Rashad Khalifa sat down in Tucson, Arizona, with a computer and a copy of the Qur'an, and started counting letters. He was Egyptian, he had a doctorate, he worked for the state of Arizona as a chemist, and he had decided that the fourteen Arabic letters used in different combinations at the openings of certain surahs were a code. What he found, or said he found, was the number nineteen. Nineteen letters in the bismillah. Multiples of nineteen everywhere he looked. He published it, and Muslims loved it, because it sounded like proof, and proof was what everybody wanted.

Then the counting stopped working. Two verses at the end of Surah at-Tawbah refused to fit the pattern. Khalifa's answer was that the verses had been inserted into the Qur'an after the Prophet's death. He removed them. A few years later he announced that he was himself a messenger of God, that the code had been sent to confirm him, and that the entire body of hadith was a satanic invention that had to be thrown out. He founded a group in Tucson and called it United Submitters International. In January 1990, he was found stabbed to death inside the mosque he had founded.

That is the extreme end. Almost nobody who repeats his arguments today has heard of him, and most would be horrified by where he ended up. But the shape of his argument is now everywhere. It has been cleaned up, made reasonable and passed around by people you love.

Mine arrived at a family lunch, from a cousin, between the salad and the tagine. He wasn't angry and he wasn't stupid. He said he had made a decision: Qur'an only. The Qur'an is the word of Allah, preserved and memorised by millions. Hadith are different. Men talking about other men talking about other men. Written down two centuries after the Prophet died. Al-Bukhari, some man from Central Asia, sifting through hundreds of thousands of stories and deciding by hand which ones were true. And then, he said, look at what actually comes out of that pile.

He had three complaints, and they're worth separating because most people push them together and then never get a proper answer to any of them.

1. The chain: All you hear from a scholar is a string of names. 'An fulan, 'an fulan, 'an fulan. Someone reported it from someone who got it from someone else. In Morocco people have a word for the whole style of it, al-'an'anah, and it is usually said with a smirk because, on the face of it, that is exactly what gossip sounds like.

2. The content: Some reports are difficult to read. Others can make a person physically recoil. Everybody knows a few because the same screenshots travel constantly.

3. The timing: Al-Bukhari lived roughly two centuries after the Prophet. Two hundred years is a long time for a sentence to travel through human mouths.

In my cousin's version, nothing happened for two hundred years. Then al-Bukhari appeared, picked up a pile of stories and made decisions. If that is what happened, my cousin was right to be suspicious. It isn't what happened.

## The morning he died

The Prophet died in Medina in June 632. He had been ill for days, but when the news spread, one of his closest companions refused to accept it. His name was Umar ibn al-Khattab. He stood in the mosque and insisted that the Prophet had gone to his Lord and would return. People let him speak because they were in shock too.

Abu Bakr arrived from outside Medina. He was the Prophet's closest friend, the man who had travelled with him when they escaped Makkah, and he would soon become the first leader of the Muslim community after him. He went to the Prophet's body, uncovered his face, kissed his forehead and covered him again. When he came out to the people he said:

'Whoever used to worship Muhammad, Muhammad has died. Whoever worships Allah, Allah is alive and does not die.'

He followed those words with a verse they already knew: 'Muhammad is only a messenger. Other messengers have passed away before him.' Umar later said his legs gave way beneath him. The verse had been in the Qur'an for years, but that morning it landed differently.

There was no Sahih al-Bukhari in 632; its compiler wouldn't be born for another 178 years. There wasn't one official volume of the Qur'an either. The revelation was complete and people had memorised it, while its written portions remained with different scribes on parchment, leather, palm stalks and other available material. Thousands of Muslims had also watched the Prophet pray, travelled with him, heard his judgements and taken their questions to him.

Those people already knew his Sunnah: the way he had taught them to live Islam. A hadith is a report that carries something he said, did or approved. The Sunnah was being practised long before the famous hadith books existed, and the people carrying those memories wouldn't live forever. They would die, move to other cities and sometimes disagree about what they had heard.

## A grandmother asks for her share

Abu Bakr became the first caliph, the name Muslims used for the leader who took charge of the community after the Prophet. He led for only two years, and much of that time was spent dealing with rebellions across Arabia. During one of the battles, many men who had memorised the Qur'an were killed. Umar worried that more of the reciters would die, so he urged Abu Bakr to gather the Qur'an into one collection.

Abu Bakr hesitated. The Prophet hadn't ordered such a collection while he was alive, and Abu Bakr was frightened of introducing a religious project of his own. He eventually agreed and placed a young scribe named Zayd ibn Thabit in charge. Zayd had written revelation for the Prophet and was trusted with the task, although he didn't sound pleased with the honour:

'By Allah, if they had ordered me to move a mountain it would not have been heavier on me than what he ordered me to do of collecting the Qur'an.'

Around the same period, a grandmother came to Abu Bakr with an inheritance question. She believed the Prophet had given grandmothers a fixed share. Abu Bakr didn't know of any such ruling and told her exactly that:

'I find nothing for you in the Book of Allah, and I do not know that the Messenger of Allah mentioned anything for you.'

Then he asked the people who had known the Prophet. A companion named al-Mughirah said he had seen the Prophet give a grandmother one sixth. Abu Bakr asked whether anybody else had been present. Another man confirmed the memory, and only then did Abu Bakr issue the ruling.

Abu Bakr's request didn't create a universal rule that every hadith needed two witnesses. Early Muslims sometimes accepted a report from one trustworthy person. But these men had buried the Prophet only months earlier, and the closest friend of the Prophet still refused to act as though a sincere Muslim couldn't misremember him.

Umar behaved in much the same way after he became the second caliph. A companion named Abu Musa once came to his door, asked permission to enter three times and left when nobody answered. Umar later demanded to know why. Abu Musa said the Prophet had taught them to leave after the third attempt, so Umar asked him to find somebody who could confirm it.

Abu Musa returned with another companion who had heard the same teaching. In one account Umar explained that he hadn't suspected Abu Musa of lying. He feared that people would become careless when speaking in the Prophet's name.

## When quotations became useful

The danger became worse about twenty-five years after the Prophet's death. Uthman, the third caliph, was murdered in his home. Ali, who became the fourth, inherited a divided community and was later assassinated too. Muslims fought over who should lead and who had been right in the conflicts that followed. Once people formed political camps, a convenient sentence attributed to the Prophet could settle an argument, damage an enemy or make one side appear chosen by Allah.

A scholar in the Iraqi city of Basra later described how Muslims responded. His name was Muhammad ibn Sirin, and he belonged to the generation born after the Prophet. Ibn Sirin said people hadn't always demanded a chain of names, but after the fitnah, the civil conflict, they began to say:

'Name your men.'

The Arabic word for that route is isnad. If a man in Basra claimed that he had received a teaching from somebody in Medina, a critic could check whether the two men were alive at the same time. Had the student ever travelled to Medina? Did the teacher's other students report the same words? If the dates didn't overlap, there was a missing person in the middle. If one student remembered a sentence differently from everybody else who attended the lessons, his version needed an explanation.

A quotation with no source leaves you with very little to investigate. Once the speaker names his teacher, and that teacher names the person before him, each name can be checked against what was publicly known about his life. Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak, a scholar from the following century, explained why Muslims became so attached to this system:

'The isnad is part of the religion. Without the isnad, whoever wished could say whatever he wished.'

## The cities became schools

Medina didn't remain the only place where the Prophet's companions taught. The Muslim world spread into Syria, Iraq, Egypt and beyond, and companions settled in the new cities. Aisha, the Prophet's wife and one of the most learned people of her generation, taught in Medina for decades. Students brought her reports they had heard from other companions, and she sometimes told them that a narrator had misunderstood or missed part of the incident. Her corrections were preserved alongside the reports she challenged. In Iraq, companions taught in Kufa and Basra, where their students spent years with them and became teachers in their own right. The books call those students the tabi'un, or followers, because they followed the companions without having met the Prophet. People in each city knew who had studied with whom and what the other students of a teacher were reporting.

Writing also began much earlier than the standard objection allows. Some companions kept personal notes. Abdullah ibn Amr was known for writing what he heard from the Prophet. In Yemen, a student named Hammam ibn Munabbih collected reports he had learned from Abu Hurayrah, a companion who spent years around the Prophet. Hammam's original first-century page isn't sitting intact in a museum today. His collection reached us through later manuscript copies and through larger books that preserved the same group of reports. Even with that qualification, the material was being written long before al-Bukhari was born.

By the end of the first Islamic century, around the year 717, a later caliph named Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz was worried that older scholars were dying. He ordered the governor of Medina to gather the reports already being taught and written in different places: 'I fear the disappearance of knowledge and the departure of the scholars.'

Books appeared well before Sahih al-Bukhari. Malik ibn Anas, a scholar in Medina, compiled the Muwatta more than a generation earlier, and scholars in Yemen, Kufa and Baghdad produced other collections. Nobody repeating the line about al-Bukhari seems to mention Malik. He spoils the image of hadith floating around unwritten for two hundred years.

## The men who checked the men

Some scholars gave most of their lives to checking narrators. They travelled to hear reports from their sources, compared what different students remembered and kept biographical records of the people whose names appeared in chains. The discipline became known as al-jarh wa al-ta'dil, criticism and accreditation, although its books aren't full of polite certificates. Was the narrator honest? Did his memory deteriorate when he grew old? Had he actually met the teacher he claimed to have heard? A man might be described as precise, truthful but prone to mistakes, weak, abandoned or a liar. The critics disagreed at times, and later scholars had to weigh those disagreements. Being religious wasn't enough. A decent Muslim could still have an unreliable memory.

One of the great hadith critics was a man named Ali ibn al-Madini. Somebody once asked him whether his own father was reliable as a narrator. Ali tried to send the question to another scholar, but they insisted. He lowered his head and then answered:

'It is the religion. He is weak.'

The exact word a narrator used also mattered. Sami'tu means 'I heard', a direct claim that the narrator heard the teacher himself. Haddathana means 'he narrated to us' and usually points to a teaching gathering. The word that attracts jokes in Morocco is 'an, which only means 'from'. 'An fulan, 'an fulan, 'an fulan: from this man, from that man, from somebody else. Hadith critics didn't treat 'from' as proof that two people had met. They checked whether a meeting was possible and whether there was evidence of an actual teaching relationship. They also identified narrators who used vague wording to hide an inconvenient gap. Al-Bukhari demanded stronger proof of a meeting than some other collectors did, which is one reason respected scholars could disagree over the same chain.

We make a cruder version of this judgement whenever a WhatsApp recording begins, 'A doctor in Spain says...' Which doctor? Which hospital? Did he make the recording, or did somebody attach his title after it had passed through fifty phones? Hadith critics asked the same kind of question with names, travel dates, teachers, students and other versions of the report in front of them. A liar could invent a chain and a careless student could copy the wrong route, so the existence of names proved nothing by itself.

## Where al-Bukhari enters

Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari was born in 810 in Bukhara, a city in what is now Uzbekistan. He grew up almost two centuries after the Prophet, and that distance sounds devastating if you imagine him travelling through random villages asking whether anybody's grandfather remembered Muhammad. In reality, he studied with recognised hadith teachers who had teachers of their own. He read earlier books, attended public lessons and compared reports that reached him by different routes. By the time he travelled through Makkah, Medina, Basra, Kufa, Baghdad and other centres of learning, those cities had been teaching hadith for generations.

He wanted to compile a particular kind of book: a collection limited to reports that met his demanding conditions. That is why the book became known as al-Sahih, the sound collection. It wasn't the first hadith book and al-Bukhari never claimed it contained every authentic report.

Traditional biographies say he worked from around six hundred thousand narrations. People repeat this as if six hundred thousand completely different sayings were piled in front of him and he rejected almost all of Islam. Hadith scholars counted the same saying again when it arrived through another chain, and they counted repeated versions too. Ten routes for one statement could enter the total as ten narrations. His finished book contains a little over seven thousand entries when repeated reports and routes are included, and roughly two and a half thousand without those repetitions. He sometimes placed the same hadith in several chapters because one part related to prayer while another part related to manners or law.

For a report to enter his collection, its chain had to be connected and the narrators had to meet a high standard of honesty and accuracy. He compared different versions and looked for hidden problems that a respectable list of names might conceal. Other collectors used standards of their own, and later scholars continued to discuss individual chains and wordings after these books became famous. Sunni Muslims respect Sahih al-Bukhari enormously. They don't believe its author received revelation or became incapable of error.

When people say hadith were written two hundred years later, they are usually giving everything in Sahih al-Bukhari the date of the finished book. Nobody would date an old family photograph by the year an aunt finally placed it in an album. The photograph had existed before the album, and a hadith could be spoken, taught and written long before al-Bukhari arranged it under a chapter heading.

The chain didn't contain two hundred people either. A report in Sahih al-Bukhari might pass through four or five named people between him and the Prophet. Some reports came through several routes. Others depended on one narrower line and therefore carried a different degree of confidence. Hadith have never formed one flat pile in which every sentence deserves the same certainty.

## The reports you can't read out loud

For most people, the chain isn't what damages their trust in hadith. It is the moment they open a translation of Sahih al-Bukhari and find the Prophet addressing women as 'deficient in intelligence and religion'. Or they read about a fly being dipped into a drink, sick men being given camel milk and urine, an adult man becoming a milk-relative of a household, or the Prophet being affected by magic.

Those reports exist. Anyone who tells you that every uncomfortable hadith is a mistranslation is buying time, and the reader can usually tell. But one appalling screenshot can't carry the conclusion that fourteen centuries of hadith scholarship were fraudulent.

What should you do instead?

Find the report itself. A sentence can circulate for centuries, appear in sermons and still have no reliable chain. Several examples above are in Sahih al-Bukhari or Sahih Muslim, the two most respected Sunni hadith collections, so authenticity alone won't make them disappear. Read the complete account and look for other reports about the same event. A screenshot won't tell you what has been cut off, whether another companion remembered a missing detail or what Muslim scholars believed the report required.

Bukhari 304 is the report about women. The English phrase sounds like a judgement on the intelligence and faith of every woman. But the Prophet explains both expressions in the report itself. The first refers to the Qur'anic rule for witnesses when a written debt is recorded. The second refers to the prayers and fasts women don't perform while menstruating because Allah has excused them from those acts of worship.

You may still dislike the wording. At least dislike what the report actually says. It doesn't say that women are half-human or incapable of thought. Aisha became one of the greatest legal authorities of the first Muslim generation. Men travelled to learn from her, and she corrected senior male companions when she believed they had misunderstood or misremembered something. The same hadith tradition preserved her corrections.

Salim's case in Sahih Muslim needs its household history. He had entered the home of Abu Hudhayfah and his wife Sahlah as an adopted son before Islam changed the legal status of adoption. He grew up in that family. Once the new rules made clear that an adopted son didn't become a biological son, his presence as a grown man created a problem for people who had never treated him as a stranger.

In Islamic law, breastfeeding can create a family bond that affects marriage and who may enter a private home. The Prophet gave Sahlah a concession through this milk kinship. Aisha believed the ruling could apply more widely, while the Prophet's other wives refused to accept that reading. Sahih Muslim records their response:

'By Allah, we do not consider this except a dispensation given by the Messenger of Allah for Salim alone.'

Most jurists agreed with them. The disagreement is sitting beside the original account in the collection, which is a strange way to run a cover-up.

The men in the camel report became ill after arriving in Medina. The Prophet sent them to the camels and told them to drink their milk and urine as treatment. It describes what was prescribed to those men; it doesn't establish a religious duty for Muslims to drink camel urine. Scholars still discussed whether the treatment came from revelation, medicine known at the time or advice specific to their condition.

The fly is harder. The report says that when one falls into a drink it should be dipped before being removed, because one wing carries disease and the other carries its cure. I don't think we help anybody by finding one weak experiment online and announcing that science has proved a miracle. We can state what the hadith says and read how scholars understood it. A scientific claim needs real evidence; our fear of a difficult answer doesn't provide it.

Magic raised a theological problem from the beginning: if the Prophet could be affected by it, what protected revelation? Sunni scholars accepted the report while holding that the effect concerned ordinary perception and didn't interfere with his delivery of Allah's message. The report itself describes Allah showing him what had happened and curing him.

There is another category that often gets pushed into the same pile: words the Prophet never said. Weak and fabricated reports are repeated in Friday sermons, printed on posters and forwarded every Ramadan because they support a beautiful lesson. Scholars compiled entire books to expose them. One later scholar, Ibn al-Jawzi, gathered roughly eighteen hundred reports he considered fabricated and arranged them by subject so other Muslims could find them. His audience was Muslim, and his concern was Muslims lying about their Prophet.

Even the popular saying 'Seek knowledge even in China' has been judged fabricated by hadith critics. People love quoting it at graduations, which doesn't improve its chain.

When I come across a report that disturbs me, I don't force myself to produce an answer before I have understood the question. I find the complete text, check how it was graded and read the other reports connected to it. Sometimes the screenshot was dishonest. In other cases the hadith is authentic, the explanation requires more work and I still don't like what I have read.

## How the Qur'an reached us

My cousin had drawn a clean line across the lunch table. The Qur'an sat on one side: divine, preserved and certain. Hadith sat on the other: human, late and muddy. The line felt obvious until I asked how the Qur'an had physically reached him.

Allah revealed the Qur'an to the Prophet, but neither of us was in the cave when the first verses came. We received it from the Muslim community that heard it, memorised it, wrote it and taught it. After reciters were killed in battle, Abu Bakr ordered Zayd ibn Thabit to gather the written material into one collection. Those sheets later remained with Hafsa, a wife of the Prophet and Umar's daughter. Years later, soldiers from different parts of the Muslim world began arguing over recitation. Uthman, the third caliph, asked Hafsa for the sheets and appointed a group led by Zayd to prepare standard copies, which he sent to the growing Muslim cities.

The preservation of the Qur'an didn't depend on four committee members alone. A whole community recited it in prayer, memorised it and taught it to the next generation. Its transmission is broader than that of an individual hadith carried through one narrow chain, and its certainty is therefore greater. Trusting the Qur'an doesn't force a Muslim to authenticate every sentence ever attributed to the Prophet. The same history already proves that human beings can preserve revelation under public checks.

Moroccans have an especially familiar example. The Qur'an commonly recited in Moroccan mosques and printed in Moroccan homes follows the reading known as Warsh 'an Nafi'. Warsh was an Egyptian reciter who studied with a teacher in Medina named Nafi'. In much of the Muslim world the common reading is Hafs 'an 'Asim. These are recognised recitations taught openly from teacher to student, with generations of public recitation around them. And there is that small word again: 'an, from. Qur'anic recitation carries far broader evidence than an individual hadith, yet nobody treats the names Warsh and Nafi' as an embarrassment attached to the Qur'an. The report about Uthman's standard copies is itself preserved in Sahih al-Bukhari, alongside the early manuscripts and public recitation that support the same history.

My cousin trusted the Muslim community to carry the Qur'an through those human routes. So did I. The disagreement was over what else that community had been capable of preserving.

## Pray Fajr for me

At lunch I asked my cousin a simpler question: using only the Qur'an, could he pray Fajr for me?

The Qur'an commands prayer constantly. It tells Muslims to stand, bow and prostrate, and it speaks about prayer at dawn and at other times of day. It doesn't contain a page saying that Fajr has two units, Maghrib has three and the midday prayer has four. It doesn't set out the order of every movement, tell you that each unit contains two prostrations or explain what to do when you forget whether you prayed three units or four. The same problem appears elsewhere. The Qur'an commands zakat, but the familiar rates and thresholds aren't gathered there. It commands Hajj, while the Prophet taught the people performing it with him to take their rites from his example.

My cousin replied, 'You can still find all of that.'

I asked where.

In the practice of Muslims, passed through families and mosques until everybody knows Fajr is two. He was right. Some parts of the Sunnah were carried so publicly in practice that a Muslim doesn't need to open Sahih al-Bukhari to discover them.

But he was already relying on something beyond the words of the Qur'an. He trusted the Prophet's example as the community had preserved it, and that is what Sunnah means.

A Qur'an-only Muslim can answer that communal practice is sufficient while individual hadith books remain unreliable. That is a more serious argument. It still leaves thousands of questions that mass practice doesn't answer, and it has to explain why the Qur'an repeatedly commands believers to obey the Messenger and take him as their example. Those commands weren't useful only while he could be seen across the mosque. Later Muslims needed a way to learn what he had taught, how he applied revelation and what he corrected when people misunderstood it.

For later Muslims, that way included the practice of the community, teachers in different cities, early written records and reports whose sources remained attached. Al-Bukhari inherited that material and organised part of it. He didn't create the Sunnah in 846.

## What the sceptic gets right

I don't trust a defence of hadith that pretends the sceptic has imagined every problem. Not every report is authentic, and Muslim scholars have said so for centuries. Preachers quote weak hadith. Beautiful fabrications return every Ramadan because nobody wants to be the miserable person who interrupts a moving reminder to ask for its source. A report can be authentic and translated badly. It can also be authentic and applied to a situation it never addressed. 'The Prophet said' and 'I found a report claiming the Prophet said' are different sentences, yet we have become far too casual about collapsing them into one.

The sceptic is also right to laugh when a chain is recited like a spell and nobody in the room understands a word of it. Arabic doesn't make an argument true. If the names matter, somebody should be able to tell us who those men were, whether they met and why the report survived criticism. Otherwise the chain has become decoration.

Where the argument goes wrong is in moving from 'some hadith are false' to 'hadith cannot preserve anything'. The first claim belongs inside hadith scholarship, where Aisha disputed reports and critics continued to challenge narrations even in famous collections.

The surviving reports don't all carry the certainty of the Qur'an, and they don't even carry the same certainty as one another. Some travelled through many routes and became widely known. Others depended on one teacher and a narrow line of students. Muslim scholars treated those reports differently because they were working with different evidence. A box marked TRUE or FALSE would be easier, but human testimony has never been that simple.

Someone eventually brought tea and the family conversation moved somewhere else. We didn't settle fourteen centuries of hadith scholarship between the tagine and a glass of mint tea. When I hear 'an fulan, 'an fulan now, I follow the names. They tell me where the report came from. If I think one of those men lied, forgot or never met the teacher he named, then I need to name him and show it.
